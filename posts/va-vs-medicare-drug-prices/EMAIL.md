## 3. Body, part one

```
Disclaimer: today’s post is an opinion piece based on data and my extensive experience studying the prescription drug supply chain as co-founder of 46brooklyn Research.

In honor of Veterans Day, I attempted to analyze the Department of Veterans Affairs’ drug pricing. I set out to compare the prices on brand-name drugs at the VA to those in Medicare Part D.

Here is the answer. On 89 of the 100 most-prescribed brand-name drugs in Medicare Part D, the VA’s prices would have saved about $13 billion, or 11%, compared with Part D’s prices: about $104 billion at the VA’s prices instead of about $117 billion at Part D’s. We based this on how many prescriptions Part D filled for each drug in 2024, priced at 2026 prices. We even created an interactive dashboard for you to compare prices on the 89 top brand-name drugs that we could match with confidence in both programs.

But here’s the rub. There are all sorts of data available in the public domain on both VA and Medicare drug prices – and none of it is what these programs pay. Part D plans get rebates, and the VA gets discounts below its published prices (different mechanics, but same difference), invalidating this analysis immediately.

Except the analysis still matters, not for the government and insurers, who get the rebates and discounts, or veterans, who only have copays for their drugs (and therefore are not subjected to inflated Compare at prices), but for the Americans in the booming Medicare Part D program (thanks to the aging of America) which now provides drug coverage for about 58 million people. Why? Because here our elderly pay for drugs based on a byzantine five- or six-tier system where insurers collect money from them based on the drug’s inflated pre-rebate price, are free to set their own prices for drugs differently across their own plans, and put off-patent generic drugs in tiers usually used for more expensive brand-name drugs.

Here is how often Part D plans put generic drugs on the tiers usually used for brand-name drugs.
```

## 5. Body, part two

```
The 5,295 Part D plans that use five or six tiers cover about 48 million people, and 76% of those people are in plans that are part of the five largest parent company insurers: UnitedHealth Group, Humana, Centene, CVS Health and Health Care Service Corporation. Across these plans, 53% of generic drug listings sit on tiers 3 to 5. That counts each strength and form of a drug once per plan, and it counts plans, not people.

So a generic placed on a brand tier is usually charged to the patient as a share of its price, and often only after the deductible is met. Before that, they pay the plan’s full price for the drug.

Americans don’t care about drug prices. They care about what they pay for drugs. Veterans, through the VA, pay a fixed copay (no more than $11 for a month’s supply, and many pay nothing) for drugs.

There is so much wrong with healthcare pricing in America that it’s hard to know where to even start. Read the full post for more interactive charts explaining what is wrong, and three recommendations.
```
